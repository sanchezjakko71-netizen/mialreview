Print & Broadcast Media ▼
Newspapers
The oldest form of print media, newspapers are published daily or weekly and cover current events, politics, sports, and local news. They follow journalistic standards such as accuracy, fairness, and timeliness. Key elements include the headline, byline, dateline, lead paragraph, and body.
Broadsheet Tabloid Editorial Op-Ed
Remember: News articles follow the inverted pyramid structure — most important info first, least important last.
Magazines
Published weekly, monthly, or quarterly, magazines target specific audiences and interests (fashion, science, sports). They feature longer, in-depth articles, high-quality photos, and ads. Unlike newspapers, they have a longer shelf life and focus on features rather than breaking news.
Feature article Periodical Target audience
Broadcast Media
Includes radio and television. It transmits audio and visual content to mass audiences simultaneously. Broadcast media is regulated by government agencies (e.g., FCC in the US) and reaches audiences in real time. Key types: news broadcasts, documentaries, entertainment shows, and public service announcements (PSA).
AM/FM Radio TV Network PSA Ratings
New Media
Refers to digital and internet-based platforms — websites, social media, podcasts, blogs, and streaming services. Characteristics: interactive, participatory, on-demand, and convergent (combines text, audio, video). Enables two-way communication between producers and audiences.
Social media Convergence User-generated content Streaming
Remember: New media differs from traditional media in that it allows audiences to become content creators, not just consumers.

Media and Information Sources ▼
Indigenous Sources
Traditional, community-based sources of knowledge passed down through oral traditions, folklore, rituals, and storytelling. These sources reflect the culture, values, and history of a local community. They are often non-written and must be respected as valid and authentic forms of information.
Oral tradition Folklore Cultural knowledge
The Library
A curated repository of information in print and digital formats. Libraries organize resources using classification systems (e.g., Dewey Decimal). Key resources: books, journals, encyclopedias, databases, and archives. Librarians assist users in information retrieval and evaluation.
Reference materials Dewey Decimal Database Archive
Mass Media
Technology used to reach a large audience simultaneously. Includes print, broadcast, and new media. Mass media shapes public opinion, culture, and values. Functions: inform, educate, entertain, persuade, and monitor society (surveillance function).
Agenda-setting Gatekeeping Framing
Remember: The 5 functions of mass media are: Surveillance, Correlation, Cultural transmission, Entertainment, and Mobilization.
The Internet
A global network enabling access to vast amounts of information. The internet allows for search engines, online databases, social networking, e-commerce, and communication. It democratizes information but also introduces risks like misinformation, fake news, and digital divide.
Search engine Misinformation Digital divide Hypertext
Evaluating Media and Information
Use the CRAAP Test to evaluate sources:

C – Currency: Is the information recent?
R – Relevance: Does it answer your question?
A – Authority: Who wrote it? Are they credible?
A – Accuracy: Is it factual and supported by evidence?
P – Purpose: Why was it created? (inform, persuade, sell?)
CRAAP Test Primary source Secondary source Credibility

Media and Information Languages ▼
Codes, Forms, and Rules
Codes are systems of signs and symbols that convey meaning. In media, codes are the techniques used to communicate messages. Forms are structural patterns (e.g., news report, documentary, advertisement). Rules are conventions that guide how media texts are constructed and interpreted.
Codes and Conventions
Technical codes — camera angles, lighting, sound, editing (e.g., close-up = intimacy; low angle = power).

Symbolic codes — setting, costume, props, facial expressions, body language.

Written codes — language style, font choice, headlines, captions.

Conventions are accepted practices within a genre (e.g., horror films use dark lighting and suspenseful music).
Denotation Connotation Semiotics Genre conventions
Remember: Denotation = literal meaning. Connotation = implied/emotional meaning. A red rose denotes a flower; it connotes love or danger.
Categorizing Codes
Codes are categorized into three main types:

1. Technical codes — how media is produced (camera work, editing, sound)
2. Symbolic/representational codes — how meaning is made through mise-en-scène
3. Written/audio codes — language, dialogue, narration, text overlays
Producers and Audiences
Producers create media content with a specific message and purpose. They make choices about codes and conventions to communicate to their target audience.

Audiences decode media using their own cultural background, experiences, and values. Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding model identifies three reading positions: dominant, negotiated, and oppositional.
Encoding Decoding Stuart Hall Reception theory
Remember: Dominant = accepts the intended message. Negotiated = partially accepts. Oppositional = rejects the message entirely.

Legal, Ethical, and Societal Issues ▼
Copyright
Copyright is a legal protection granted to creators of original works (text, images, music, film). It gives the creator exclusive rights to reproduce, distribute, and profit from their work. Copyright is automatic — no registration needed. Duration varies by country (commonly life of author + 70 years).
Intellectual property Public domain Creative Commons
Fair Use
Fair use (or fair dealing) is a legal doctrine allowing limited use of copyrighted material without permission, for purposes such as education, commentary, criticism, news reporting, or parody. Four factors determine fair use:

1. Purpose — educational/non-commercial vs. commercial
2. Nature — factual vs. creative work
3. Amount — how much was used
4. Effect — does it harm the market value?
Parody Criticism Attribution
Remember: Fair use is a defense, not a right. Always cite your source even when fair use applies.
Plagiarism
Plagiarism is presenting someone else's ideas, words, or work as your own without proper attribution. It is both an ethical violation and, in many cases, a legal one. Types include: direct plagiarism (copy-paste), self-plagiarism (reusing your own old work), mosaic plagiarism (mixing sources), and accidental plagiarism (improper citation).
Citation Paraphrase Academic integrity
Remember: To avoid plagiarism — Quote, Paraphrase, or Summarize — and always cite the source.
Netiquette
Netiquette (internet etiquette) refers to the rules of respectful and responsible behavior online. Core principles:

• Be respectful — no flaming, trolling, or cyberbullying
• Protect privacy — don't share others' personal info
• Use proper language — avoid ALL CAPS (perceived as shouting)
• Give credit — cite sources, don't plagiarize
• Verify before sharing — fact-check to avoid spreading misinformation
Cyberbullying Digital citizenship Online privacy Flaming
Remember: The Golden Rule of Netiquette — Treat others online as you would in real life.
